This homework extends Lab 4 (random walks, LLN and CLT): the same m hackers and n systems, now with a score in [0, 10] for every attempt.

Ex. 1 — Welford's recurrence formulas

1.1 Knuth's recurrence for the mean

Let Mk = (x1 + … + xk)/k. Split off the last term and use (k−1)Mk−1 = x1 + … + xk−1:

Mk = ((k−1)·Mk−1 + xk) / k = Mk−1 + (xk − Mk−1) / k,    M1 = x1

The new mean is the old mean corrected by a fraction 1/k of the "surprise" xk − Mk−1. Two facts used below follow directly:

(A)  k·(Mk − Mk−1) = xk − Mk−1
(B)  (k−1)·Mk−1 = k·Mk − xk

1.2 Welford's recurrence for the sum of squares

Define the sum of squared deviations SSk = Σi≤k (xi − Mk)². Expanding the square, SSk = Σi≤k xi² − k·Mk². Following the same approach as for the mean, look at the increment:

SSk − SSk−1 = xk² − k·Mk² + (k−1)·Mk−1²
  = xk² − k·Mk² + Mk−1·(k·Mk − xk)            by (B)
  = xk·(xk − Mk−1) − Mk·k·(Mk − Mk−1)
  = xk·(xk − Mk−1) − Mk·(xk − Mk−1)          by (A)
  = (xk − Mk−1)·(xk − Mk)
SSk = SSk−1 + (xk − Mk−1)·(xk − Mk),    SS1 = 0

The increment is the product of the deviation from the old mean and the deviation from the new mean. It is never negative (both factors have the same sign, since Mk lies between Mk−1 and xk), so SSk can only grow. An equivalent form, using (A), is SSk = SSk−1 + (k−1)/k · (xk − Mk−1)².

1.3 Recurrence for the variance

The sample (unbiased) variance is sk² = SSk/(k−1). Substituting SSk = (k−1)sk² and SSk−1 = (k−2)sk−1² gives a recurrence with exactly the shape of Knuth's formula (old value + correction / count):

sk² = sk−1² + [ (xk − Mk−1)(xk − Mk) − sk−1² ] / (k−1),    k ≥ 2
σ̂k² = σ̂k−1² + [ (xk − Mk−1)(xk − Mk) − σ̂k−1² ] / k       (population form, σ̂k² = SSk/k)

In practice one updates SS and divides only when the variance is needed.

1.4 Code

The core logic is three lines: one deviation, Knuth's update, Welford's update.

// JavaScript (labs/static/labs/lab5/sim.js)
k += 1;
const d = x - mean;      // deviation from the OLD mean
mean += d / k;           // Knuth
ss += d * (x - mean);    // Welford: (x − M_{k−1})(x − M_k)
// variance = ss / (k − 1)
# Python
def welford(xs):
    k, mean, ss = 0, 0.0, 0.0
    for x in xs:
        k += 1
        d = x - mean
        mean += d / k
        ss += d * (x - mean)
    return mean, ss / (k - 1)

Why not the textbook one-pass formula (Σx² − (Σx)²/n)/(n−1)? It subtracts two huge, nearly equal numbers. Welford only ever squares deviations, which are small, so it stays accurate while reading the data once (an online, streaming algorithm). Try a large offset below:

With offset 0 all three agree (variance 81). With offset 1e9 the variance should not change; Welford is still correct to about 7 digits, while the naive formula returns 0 because x² ≈ 1018 exceeds the 253 precision of a double.

Ex. 2 — Sampling mean and variance

2.1 Model

There are m hackers, each attacking n systems. Each attempt receives a score X ∈ [0, 10], i.i.d. from a fixed distribution with mean μ and variance σ². A hacker's n scores are one sample of size n; Welford's formulas give its sampling mean x̄ and sampling variance s² = SSn/(n−1). The m hackers are m independent repetitions of the same sampling experiment.

Conclusions

What the comparisons show

  • The average of the sampling means matches μ for every n, even n = 2: x̄ is an unbiased estimator, E[x̄] = μ. Any single hacker can be far off; on average there is no systematic error.
  • The average of the sampling variances matches σ² for every n, because s² divides by n−1 (Bessel's correction). Dividing by n instead gives on average (n−1)/n·σ²: half of σ² at n = 2, 10% low at n = 10 (last column). The reason is that the deviations are taken from x̄, which is fitted to the same data and is closer to them than μ is: one degree of freedom is used up.
  • The residual gaps are of the size predicted by the standard errors (|z| mostly below 2). They shrink with m, not with n: m controls how well the simulation measures the expectation. The rows use the same trajectories, so their z values are correlated.

Why this matters in inferential statistics

  • In practice μ and σ² are unknown and we see one sample (one hacker). The m hackers simulate the frequentist thought experiment of repeating the sampling many times; the histograms are the sampling distributions of x̄ and s².
  • Unbiasedness justifies using x̄ and s² as point estimates of μ and σ².
  • Var[x̄] = σ²/n, estimated by s²/n, gives the standard error s/√n. Together with the CLT (the histogram of x̄ becomes normal) it yields confidence intervals x̄ ± t·s/√n and hypothesis tests (e.g. "is this hacker's mean score above 5?"). Inference on variances (χ² intervals, F tests) relies on the distribution of s².
  • Welford makes all this computable online: a monitoring system can keep, for every attacker, the running mean and variance of their scores in O(1) memory and update them on each new attempt.

Effect of n (systems per hacker)

  • The centre does not move: the average of x̄ stays at μ and that of s² at σ² for every n. Larger n does not remove bias (there is none); it removes noise.
  • Sampling means: Var[x̄] = σ²/n, so the spread of the hackers' means falls like 1/√n (slope −½ on the log–log plot). Going from 10 to 1000 systems makes it 10 times narrower. The histogram becomes more and more normal (CLT), even for the skewed zero-inflated scores.
  • Sampling variances: Var[s²] ≈ (μ₄ − σ⁴)/n for large n, also decaying like 1/√n in SD, but starting much higher. For small n the distribution of s² is strongly right-skewed (many hackers well below σ², a few far above; s² can even be 0) and the variance is harder to estimate than the mean. Heavy tails (large μ₄, as in the zero-inflated model) make it worse.
  • Both estimators are therefore consistent: each individual hacker's x̄ and s² converge to μ and σ² (the running plots), which is the Law of Large Numbers of Lab 4 applied to X and to (X−μ)².
